## What It Is
The central idea is what the song is trying to say and the angle it looks from. Try to answer in one sentence: "this song is about what, seen from where." For example "a love that is over, seen by someone who still keeps the other person's things", not just "love".

Berklee's lyric-writing course that starts from the title teaches choosing the central idea first and then developing the sections around it [R1]. A clear idea makes it easier to decide which details stay and which go.

A song with a clear idea and a song that is deliberately open to several readings differ. The first knows what it says and chooses to say it directly or indirectly. The second knows what the fixed core is and leaves the meaning open around it. Good ambiguity has a core under it; ambiguity that is only an undecided idea feels empty.

## Why It Works
- Listeners take in a song faster when every section feels like it speaks about one thing, even from several sides.
- A specific idea gives the details a job. An image or event that does not serve the idea feels like an extra.
- Once the idea is known, developing the song is easier because you can see which view or fact has not been said yet.
- A song that is open on purpose needs something the listener can hold, such as a repeating image or one feeling that stays, so that it can open without coming loose.

## How to Use
Three steps: write the idea in one sentence, decide whether to say it directly or indirectly, then walk through the sections and ask what each adds to the idea.

### one-sentence | The one-sentence test
- Write "this song is about ___ seen from ___". If you cannot fill it in, the idea has not been chosen yet.
- If several answers fit equally, choose one as the core and let the others be a second layer.

### angle | Choose the angle
- One subject can be told from several places: the person who left, the one left behind, a witness, or someone years later.
- A specific angle usually makes the song different from others about the same subject.

### support | Let the details serve the idea
- Ask of every section: "what does this add to the sentence?"
- A section that adds nothing need not always be cut. It may have another job (setting a mood, a pause), but you should know what it is.

### open | Open to interpretation on purpose
- Decide the fixed core (one image or one feeling) and let the rest be read in more than one way.
- Check that two listeners can read it differently while both readings still fit the words that are really there.

## Examples
The lines below were written for teaching. The subject is the same, "rain", with different ideas.

- A clear idea: "about still keeping her umbrella, seen by someone who does not dare return it" → "ร่มสีเขียวยังแขวนอยู่หลังประตู / ฝนตกกี่หนฉันก็ไม่หยิบมันไป" (the green umbrella still hangs behind the door; however often it rains I do not take it)
- A clear idea, another angle: "rain seen by someone who never had an umbrella to share" → "ฉันยืนใต้ชายคาร้านเดิม / มองคนสองคนแบ่งร่มกันเดินผ่านไป" (I stand under the same shop awning, watching two people share an umbrella walk by)
- Open with a core: the images of a quiet roof and a blue light repeat; a listener may hear loneliness, waiting or peace, but the images still point to a room with nobody in it
- Open with no core: "ฝนก็คือฝน ลมก็คือลม ทุกอย่างคือความรู้สึก" (rain is rain, wind is wind, everything is feeling) → it can be read any way because it says nothing in particular

## Common Pitfalls
- An idea so wide ("love", "life") that it helps decide nothing.
- Thinking that being vague on purpose is the same as being deep, when the idea has not actually been chosen.
- Keeping a beautiful detail that has nothing to do with the idea because you like the image too much.
- Putting several ideas in one song with no main one.
- You do not have to state the idea outright in the lyrics. Knowing it is already a help.

## Try It Yourself
(1) Write your song's idea in one sentence, "about ___ seen from ___". (2) Go through each section and write half a line on what it adds to that sentence. (3) For a section that adds nothing, decide what job it should have, or keep it for another reason. (4) Try writing the same idea from another angle and see which lines of the song would change.
